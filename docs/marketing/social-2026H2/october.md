## Week 1 — Attention Is Life Support

**LinkedIn**

Every post on Swimchain is dying. On purpose.

Content on our network has a half-life of seven days. If nobody engages with it, it fades — mathematically, predictably, gone. If people keep engaging, it lives.

We call the principle "attention is life support."

Most platforms treat content as inventory: every hot take, every argument, every bad day preserved forever and resurfaced whenever it drives a click. The archive becomes a landfill and a weapon at the same time.

Communities don't work that way. Healthy communities forget. The inside joke survives because people keep telling it. The flame war dies because nobody feeds it.

You can watch this happen at swimchain.io/reef — a coral reef that lives on-chain, where every tile is a post. Tend it and it grows. Walk away and it fades back to open water.

Forgetting isn't a bug in human memory. It's how groups stay healthy. We just built a network that agrees.

What would you say differently if you knew it wouldn't be held against you forever?

## Week 1 — Forgetting Is a Feature

**LinkedIn**

The internet never learned to forget. We built a network that does.

Human communities have always run on decay. Stories that matter get retold. Grudges that don't get dropped. That's not data loss — that's health.

Platforms broke this. They keep everything, because everything is inventory. Your 2014 opinion is stored, indexed, and waiting, whether or not anyone — including you — still stands behind it.

On Swimchain, content decays with a seven-day half-life unless people engage with it. Nothing survives by default. Things survive because a community keeps them alive.

The result isn't an empty network. It's a current one: everything still standing is standing because someone cares that it is. The Daily Drift — a newspaper served entirely from the chain, with no server behind it — works because news is supposed to be new. And at the other end, reference material lives by the same rule: our on-chain Wiki (including a full Minecraft wiki) lasts exactly as long as readers keep using it.

Care is the storage layer.

## Week 2 — Thirty Seconds of Meaning It

**LinkedIn**

Posting on Swimchain costs my computer real work — a few seconds to about thirty of actual computation, every single time.

That sounds like a downside. It's the entire point.

When posting is free, everything gets posted. Spam is rational. Outrage farming is rational. A bot posting ten thousand times an hour is rational. The economics of "free" built the feeds we all hate.

Add a small, unavoidable cost — proof-of-work, paid in electricity and patience — and the math flips. Spam becomes economically pointless. A bot farm burns real resources for nothing. Ad campaigns don't pencil out. And a human, waiting those thirty seconds, gets one last moment to ask: do I actually mean this?

Friction isn't a failure of design. Frictionlessness was.

We'll say it plainly: Swimchain is harder than scrolling Instagram. Slower to post, slower to grow. We think that's what a place worth saying things in feels like.

Feel the cost of your own words — start a thread in the Forum on the desktop app, which runs your own node (free, Windows): swimchain.io/download

Would you say less if every post cost you thirty seconds? Would that be bad?

#opensource #socialmedia #decentralization

**Facebook**

On our network, posting isn't instant. Your computer has to do a little real work first — a few seconds, sometimes up to thirty.

Why would anyone build that? Because when posting costs nothing, spam is free and bots run wild. When it costs a little something, only people with something to say bother.

Thirty seconds is nothing to a human and ruinous to a spam operation. That's the whole trick.

## Week 3 — There Is No Algorithm to Blame

**LinkedIn**

Nobody decides what you see on Swimchain. Not us — there is no us. No company, no ranking team, no engagement model. Your feed is assembled by your own node, running on your own machine, following rules you can read.

Think about what "the algorithm" actually is on other platforms: a stranger's business model, executed against your attention. It decides which friend you drift from and which outrage finds you at 11pm. You can't inspect it, can't configure it, can't refuse it.

On Swimchain, the Feed app builds your timeline from what YOUR node knows — the spaces you follow, the people you engage with. And every rule a node enforces is written down: thirteen published protocol specs, all open source. If you don't like a behavior, you can read exactly why it happens.

It's a quieter feed. Less serendipitous outrage, more of what you actually chose. Fair warning: it may feel boring for the first week. We think that feeling is withdrawal.

Your attention, allocated by you. Radical concept, apparently.

If a machine has to build your feed, shouldn't it be one you own? swimchain.io

## Week 4 — Nothing to Buy, No Plug to Pull

**LinkedIn**

Swimchain cannot be acquired. There is nothing to acquire.

No company. No servers to change hands. No user database to be an asset in a sale. The network is its users: every person runs a node, and the nodes ARE the platform.

We watched a decade of beloved communities get bought, "aligned with strategy," and hollowed out. The lesson we drew wasn't "pick better owners." It was: a community's home shouldn't have an owner at all.

So we wrote the rules down where no one can quietly change them — thirteen published protocol specs covering everything a node enforces, from identity to content decay to sponsorship. All open source. Identities are pseudonymous and cryptographic: you are your keys, not a row in someone's database.

The social network that can't be bought, sold, or shut down — not as a slogan, as an architecture.

This month we covered why our content decays, why posting costs real work, why there's no algorithm, and why we're fine not being for everyone. It all reduces to one property: nobody owns it, so nobody can ruin it.
